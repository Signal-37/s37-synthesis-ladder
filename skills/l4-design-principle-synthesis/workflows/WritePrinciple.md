# WritePrinciple Workflow

Craft a single L4 Design Principle from a named cluster of L3 Insights. This workflow covers the move from declarative insight claims to a standing behavioral rule — the short, active, plain statement that governs how the organization acts in any relevant decision.

## When to Use

When you have a named insight cluster and need to write the principle. Can be invoked from SynthesizePrinciples or called directly when the user hands you a specific cluster to develop.

---

## The Critical Distinction

L4 Design Principles govern *how* the organization acts — in every decision, regardless of context.

| Level | Form | Used as |
|-------|------|---------|
| **L3 Insight** | "Buyers judge the brand by how the dealer treats them, and the brand does not design or check that experience." | Plain statement of what is true |
| **L4 OA** | "How might we design the customer's relationship with the brand from the dealer visit through the years after purchase?" | Designed territory for action |
| **L4 Principle** | "Design what buyers experience at the dealer" | Standing rule governing all experience design decisions |
| **L5 Framework** | [Organizing structure that holds all of the above] | Strategic document |

The principle does not name a program. It names the behavioral commitment that should govern all programs in its domain. A principle can be applied to any future decision — it doesn't expire when a specific campaign or initiative ends.

---

## Before Writing: Identify the Decision Domain

Before drafting a single word, identify what type of decisions this principle will govern. A principle that doesn't govern a recognizable class of decisions is not operational.

Ask:
- What kind of decision would a leader in this organization make where this principle applies?
- Would this principle be relevant in the decision types this domain actually faces? Draw the candidates from the domain's decision-type list in `DomainScope.md` — a budget discussion / campaign brief / dealer meeting / community partnership for brand/design; a roadmap call / feature spec / design review for product/UX; a budget-headcount / process-change / role decision for organizational; a funding decision / program-design / rule-making choice for policy/advocacy.
- Can you imagine this principle being cited explicitly during a real meeting — "Before we approve this, let's ask..."?

If the decision domain is unclear, the principle may be too abstract. Either narrow the source cluster or identify that this insight is better captured by an OA than a principle.

## Writing Order

The title is written last (the researcher, 2026-11-07, carried from the L3 rule of 2026-11-05/06). Start from what the insights show people do or say, and what the organization must therefore always do.

**1. Write "The principle."** In 1–3 plain sentences, state the full operational demand: what the organization must always do, ask, or test. Begin with the verb. End with the specific operational implication. Rules: `PrincipleFormat.md` → Plain Writing in the Body.

This is the wall-card version: short enough to display, specific enough to govern.

**2. Write "What it means."** Name the structural problem — often two related failure modes with a shared root. Then name what the principle demands as the alternative. This section proves the principle is more than a slogan: it has specific organizational meaning that would be violated by identifiable decisions.

3–5 sentences. Use the structure: "Two [failures/problems] share the same root. [Name both.] The fix is the same: [the principle's demand]."

**3. Write "Why it comes from the research."** Name participants by name. Use direct quotes where they exist — where none exists, name the behavioral evidence; never construct or approximate a quote. Narrative additions carry `(illustrative framing)`. Connect the evidence to the principle's demand — the evidence should make the principle feel inevitable, not just plausible.

2–4 sentences. Tight. Every sentence should add a new participant, quote, or finding. The re-grounding test: to ignore this principle, the organization would have to look these specific people in the eye and decide their experience doesn't matter.

**4. Write "How to apply it."** Name the decision types this governs. Write the specific test question(s) in quotation marks — the actual question a decision-maker should ask before approving any relevant program, budget, or campaign.

2–4 sentences. The test question must have a clear directional answer when the principle is being honored and a clear warning signal when it is not.

**5. Write "What it prevents."** Name the specific failure mode precisely. Often two related failure modes (the twin traps). The failure mode should be specific enough to recognize if it is currently happening.

1–2 sentences. Precise. Connects back to the structural problem named in "What it means."

**6. Write "What it rejects."** Name the specific alternative approach, program type, or organizational move the principle explicitly refuses. Distinct from "What it prevents": prevents = the failure mode; rejects = the alternative strategy that produces it. Specific enough that a planning-session participant would recognize it as something that has been proposed or considered.

1–2 sentences. The rejected move should be something the organization might plausibly choose — not an obvious bad decision.

**7. Write the title last.** Name in plain words what "The principle" demands. Rules: `PrincipleFormat.md` → The Principle Title Test.
- Short (usually 4–10 words; when short and plain conflict, plain wins)
- Begins with an imperative verb
- Plain: a reader with no project context can say what it asks the organization to do, on first read
- Governing: a decision-maker can apply it to a real choice

Draft 2–3 candidates and keep the one a cold reader would understand soonest, provided it still names the governing logic (where? how? for whom?). If none states the demand without its body, the principle may be too broad, or two principles may be conflated: revise the cluster, not the wording.

**8. List source insights.** All contributing L3 insights with IDs and titles.

## The Principle Title — Construction Patterns

Patterns that produce plain principle titles. Every pattern still has to pass the first-read test: a reader with no project context can say what the title asks the organization to do. Before and after examples: `PrincipleFormat.md` → The Principle Title Test.

**1. Verb + where clause:** Names what to do and the governing condition.
- "Spend where buyers decide which brand to trust" (not just "invest in community")
- The where-clause carries the insight: not any community, but the places buyers decide

**2. Verb + object:** Direct. Names what the principle governs.
- "Design what buyers experience at the dealer"
- Name the object in full. "Design the dealer" is shorter, but a cold reader has to ask what designing a dealer means.

**3. Two demands joined by "and":** Only when the two are one move.
- "State what the brand stands for, and show it beyond the product"
- If the two demands govern different decisions, write two principles. Do not use a semicolon pairing for rhythm.

**4. Verb + object + qualifier:** Adds the governing condition or constraint.
- "Design the relationship after the purchase, not just the sale"
- Use when the common default (designing only the sale) is the failure the principle prevents, and say the default plainly.

Avoid categorical reframes ("Welcome as a brand act"): they ask the reader to decode a reclassification. Say the action plainly: "Treat welcoming new buyers as part of the brand".

---

## "What It Means" — The Structural Problem Pattern

Most principles respond to a compound failure: two related breakdowns that share a single root cause. Naming both failures and their shared root is what gives "What it means" its persuasive force.

Structure:
1. Name failure mode 1: "First: [what happens when the principle is ignored in context A]"
2. Name failure mode 2: "Second: [what happens when the principle is ignored in context B]"
3. Name the shared root: "Both share the same root: [the organizational assumption or gap that produces both]"
4. Name the fix: "The fix is the same: [what the principle demands]"

This structure proves that the principle is not arbitrary — it is the one response that addresses both failure modes simultaneously.

---

## "How To Apply It" — The Test Question

The test question is the operational core of the principle. It transforms an organizational commitment into a replicable decision ritual.

**Writing the test question:**
- Begins with "Does this..." or "Will this..." or "What [specific thing] does this..."
- Names the specific outcome the principle requires
- Has a binary directionality: yes = principle honored, no = principle violated
- Is short enough to ask in a meeting without reading from a document

**Test for the test question:** Ask a colleague to read it. Can they immediately answer yes or no about a real decision they faced recently? If the answer is "it depends" without further clarification, the question is too abstract.

## Principle Quality Check

Before finalizing:
- [ ] Title written last; short, active, plain, governing: passes all four title tests in `PrincipleFormat.md` → The Principle Title Test
- [ ] Every section, including the test questions, meets `PrincipleFormat.md` → Plain Writing in the Body
- [ ] "The principle" states the operational demand (not just what is true)
- [ ] "What it means" names two related failure modes and their shared root
- [ ] "What it means" names what the principle demands as the alternative
- [ ] "Why it comes from the research" names participants by name
- [ ] "Why it comes from the research" uses direct quotes where they exist
- [ ] "How to apply it" names decision types AND includes test question(s) in quotation marks
- [ ] Test question has a clear directional answer
- [ ] "What it prevents" names a specific, recognizable failure mode
- [ ] "What it rejects" names the specific alternative approach the principle vetoes — specific enough that a planning-session participant would recognize it as something proposed or considered
- [ ] Source insights listed with IDs and titles
- [ ] YAML frontmatter complete: project, principle_id, synthesis_level, source_insights, governs (from the domain's DomainScope vocabulary), synthesis_date, domain
