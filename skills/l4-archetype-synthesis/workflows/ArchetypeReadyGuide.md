# ArchetypeReadyGuide Workflow

Designs research instruments that will produce data sufficient for L4 archetype synthesis. Two scenarios:

- **Scenario A — Dedicated archetype research:** builds a full discussion guide from scratch, structured around the archetype data requirements matrix
- **Scenario B — Additive:** audits an existing discussion guide, produces a gap report, and generates specific insertions with placement guidance

Output is returned inline for review, then written to the project's research directory on confirmation.

## Step 2: Context Intake and Scenario Detection

Confirm:

1. **Scenario:** Is an existing discussion guide already in hand? → **B (Additive)**. No guide yet? → **A (Dedicated)**
2. **Client / project name**
3. **Category and domain** — what product, service, or experience is the research about?
4. **Research phase** — foundational (generative, no stimulus) or descriptive (with stimulus, prototype, or concept)?
5. **Participant profile** — who will be interviewed? How many participants? Any screener criteria?
6. **Preliminary hypotheses about behavioral variation** — what dimensions of difference across participants are expected? (If none, say so — the matrix will help surface them)
7. **Synthesis timeline** — will archetype synthesis run during this project, or is this research banking data for a future project?

**For Scenario B only:** Confirm the location of the existing guide and load it fully before proceeding.

**Data gate:** If the research phase is Evaluative (reactions to a specific stimulus, prototype test, A/B test), flag immediately. Evaluative research produces stimulus-anchored data. It can surface attitude variation, but it will not produce the behavior-in-natural-context accounts that archetype synthesis requires. Proceed only if foundational fieldwork accompanies the evaluative study, or the client understands the confidence ceiling.

## Step 3: Archetype Data Requirements Matrix

This matrix is the core of the workflow. It translates L4 archetype synthesis requirements into question design principles. Apply it in Step 4 to build or audit the guide.

**Thirteen requirements:**

### R1 — Behavioral accounts (not attitudes)

**What synthesis needs:** L2 themes are built from descriptions of what participants *did* — behaviors, workarounds, adaptations, avoidances — not from what they say they prefer or value. Attitude clusters and behavioral archetypes are different things.

**Question design principle:** Open-ended behavioral accounts. Start with "Walk me through..." or "Tell me about a time when...". Anchor to a specific, recent instance. Follow up with "What did you actually do?" not "How did you feel?"

**Anti-patterns:** Rating scales; "How important is X to you?"; "Do you prefer X or Y?"; "Would you say you're someone who...?"

**Example frames:**
- "Walk me through the last time you [category behavior]. What actually happened?"
- "Tell me about a specific time when you [decision/purchase/action]. Take me there."
- "What do you actually do when [X]? Step by step."

---

### R2 — Utility spectrum data (Functional ↔ Emotional)

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of whether the participant's dominant motivation is functional/logical/product-focused or emotional/experience-seeking. This is frequently masked — cycling culture, for example, valorizes functional reasons while emotional and aesthetic motivations are often more determinative.

**Question design principle:** Force a ranking or prioritization, then probe the actual sequence of events against that ranking. Watch for self-correction (the participant who ranks "performance" first but then reveals aesthetics drove the decision). Ask about what they *remember most* from a purchase moment.

**Example frames:**
- "If you had to pick the single most important thing about [X], what comes first? ... Now walk me through the last time you decided on [X] — did that thing actually drive the decision, or was something else pulling?"
- "What's the thing that surprised you most about your decision — something you didn't expect to matter?"
- "At the moment you committed, what was it?"

---

### R3 — Swagger spectrum data (Leader ↔ Follower)

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of whether the participant self-directs decisions confidently, or needs external guidance (expert, peer, dealer) to feel ready to commit.

**Question design principle:** Ask when they felt confident enough to act, and what got them there. Listen for whether that threshold was internal (they simply knew) or external (someone else confirmed it was right).

**Example frames:**
- "At what point did you feel confident enough to [commit/buy/choose]? What got you to that point?"
- "Was there a person whose opinion you needed before you could feel good about it? Who, and why them?"
- "Did you ever feel like you were done deciding but then second-guessed it? What pulled you back?"

---

### R4 — Spontaneity spectrum data (Routine ↔ Spontaneous)

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of whether the participant's approach is planned and deliberate, or contextual and impulsive. Timeline and trigger questions surface this.

**Question design principle:** Ask for the timeline from first awareness to commitment. Ask what finally made it happen. The "what finally made it happen" answer often reveals whether the decision was executed on a deliberate plan or triggered by an external event.

**Example frames:**
- "How long had you been thinking about [X] before you actually [acted]?"
- "What finally made it happen — was there a specific moment, or did it just gradually feel right?"
- "Do you tend to plan [this type of decision] well in advance, or do things just come together when the moment's right?"

---

### R5 — Motivation spectrum data (Self ↔ Validation)

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of whether the purchase/decision is driven by internal satisfaction or by social validation and sharing. This is often revealed *after* the purchase, not during.

**Question design principle:** Ask what happened after — who they told, what reaction they sought or received. The participant who tells no one is at the Self end; the participant who texts photos immediately and waits for responses is at the Validation end.

**Example frames:**
- "After [X], who was the first person you told? What did you tell them?"
- "Did you share it anywhere? Post about it?"
- "How did it feel when [person] saw/heard about it? Did their reaction matter?"

---

### R6 — Situational trigger data

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of moment-level factors that shift behavior — time pressure, occasion type, who they're with, functional vs. emotional mode for that occasion. Required for the "Situational triggers" section of each archetype card.

**Question design principle:** Ask explicitly about when their usual approach changes. Present two contrasting scenarios and ask what's different about their behavior in each.

**Example frames:**
- "Is there a situation where you'd approach [X] very differently from usual? What changes, and what triggers that?"
- "When you're [in a hurry / shopping for yourself / shopping for an event / with someone else], does your approach to [X] change?"
- "Has there been a time when your normal approach was exactly backwards from what you usually do? What was going on?"

---

### R7 — Developmental trigger data

**What synthesis needs:** Evidence of life events that have durably shifted the participant's relationship with the category — not gradual preference drift, but event-anchored transitions (injury, return to sport, first serious purchase, becoming a parent, changing careers). Required for the "Developmental triggers" section of each archetype card. Phase of life is not a demographic proxy — life events predict it; age does not.

**Question design principle:** Ask about changes over time and what drove them. Listen for event-anchored language ("and then I..." / "that's when...") rather than gradual drift ("over time I sort of..."). Probe for the before/after.

**Example frames:**
- "Has your relationship with [category] changed significantly in the past few years? What drove that shift?"
- "Is there a moment that changed how you think about [X]?"
- "What was your approach to [X] five years ago? How is it different now — and what accounts for that difference?"
- "Has anything happened in your life that changed how you engage with [category]?"

---

### R8 — Fluidity evidence

**What synthesis needs:** Data that tests whether the participant's behavioral mode is stable across contexts or shifts with situation. Without this, archetypes cannot be declared journey-specific or person-level. The most useful fluidity data comes from presenting two clearly different contexts and asking the participant to compare their behavior in each.

**Question design principle:** Name two contrasting contexts explicitly. Ask for behavioral comparison, not stated preference. Listen for whether the participant describes meaningfully different behavior or says "pretty much the same."

**Example frames:**
- "When you're [buying a frame vs. buying components], do you approach those decisions the same way, or very differently? Walk me through each."
- "Think about [a high-stakes decision vs. a routine one in this category] — what's the same and what changes?"
- "Is there a context in which you're [the type of buyer/rider/participant they've described] vs. a context where you're something else entirely?"

---

### R9 — First-contact and conversion evidence

**What synthesis needs:** Data about the specific moment the participant first encountered and committed to the brand or category. This is the raw material for archetypes grounded in embodied loyalty and pre-rational commitment — behavioral modes where the conversion happens below the level of conscious reasoning and is stored as kinesthetic or aesthetic memory rather than argument. The most useful data here is sensory, specific, and often vivid — participants remember these moments in detail. Ask for the experience, not the reasoning.

**Question design principle:** Take them back to the specific moment. Ask for sensory and narrative detail. Resist asking "why" — it invites rationalization after the fact. Ask "what was that like?" and "what happened next?"

**Example frames:**
- "Take me back to when you first [encountered / committed to / fell for X]. Where were you? What happened?"
- "What's your clearest memory of the moment you knew you wanted [X]? What was happening around you?"
- "When did [X] go from something you knew about to something you had to have? What changed?"

---

### R10 — Trust architecture data

**What synthesis needs:** Data about who the participant trusts for information, recommendations, and validation in this category — and how that trust operates. Required for archetypes grounded in peer-mediated and AI-mediated decision-making — behavioral modes where the purchase decision is delegated to a disinterested source rather than driven by the brand or the buyer's independent judgment. Also surfaces the peer advocacy network and AI-mediated discovery behaviors that carry design implications.

**Question design principle:** Ask about the full trust chain. Start with how they found out about [X], then work forward to who influenced the decision and how. Ask specifically about AI tools — this sub-behavior is often volunteered only if asked.

**Example frames:**
- "Before you [committed to / purchased / started using X], where did you go for information? Walk me through that process."
- "Whose opinion counted most in this decision? Why that person?"
- "Do you ever use AI tools — ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, anything like that — to research [category]?"
- "Is there someone whose [category] choices you just trust — where their using something is enough for you?"

---

### R11 — Negative case elicitation

**What synthesis needs:** Data about when the participant's usual approach broke down — times they behaved differently from their pattern. Negative cases are the primary mechanism for refining cluster definitions in the recursive derivation step. Without them, archetypes are over-fitted to the consistent behavior and miss the variation that defines their edges.

**Question design principle:** Ask for exceptions explicitly. Frame it as learning ("helps me understand where the pattern breaks"). Ask for specific instances. Do not accept "I can't think of one" without probing — there is almost always an exception.

**Example frames:**
- "Has there been a time when your usual approach to [X] completely didn't work — when you did something totally different? What happened?"
- "Can you think of a purchase [decision / engagement] in this category that went differently from your usual? What was going on?"
- "When would you NOT [typical behavior]? Has that happened?"

---

### R12 — KI-generating behavioral specifics

**What synthesis needs:** Data that produces observable behavioral signals — what a researcher, designer, or data analyst could see or detect in the field. Key Indicators are the bridge between an archetype's psychological description and design-testable hypotheses. They are built from specific physical behaviors described in the research.

**Question design principle:** Ask for physical behaviors, not feelings. Ask for what they actually do step-by-step. "What do you do?" not "How do you approach?" Listen for verbs (take photos, call the shop, look up specs on the train, put the bike on the trainer first) not adjectives (careful, enthusiastic, thorough).

**Example frames:**
- "What do you actually do when you're [researching / deciding / after buying X]? Walk me through it, step by step."
- "If I were watching you [engage with X], what would I see?"
- "What's the first thing you physically do when [situation that triggers category behavior]?"

---

### R13 — Adoption-curve positioning

**What synthesis needs:** Data about where the participant sits in the adoption curve for this category — early adopter, mainstream, or laggard. `SynthesizeArchetypes` Step 9 (Fluidity Check) requires declaring which archetype maps to early adopters, and the beachhead archetype (the early-adopter archetype) is the natural first target for any new offering. Without this data, the synthesizer must infer adoption-curve position from context, which is less reliable.

**Question design principle:** Ask directly about timing relative to peers and about the participant's stance toward new things in the category. Early adopters in a category typically know they are early; they can articulate it. The key contrast is: do they seek out new offerings before peers, or wait until something is proven?

**Example frames:**
- "When something new comes out in [category] — a new model, a new format, a new brand — where do you typically land? Are you someone who tries it early, or do you wait to see how it goes?"
- "Can you think of a time you were one of the first people you knew to [adopt something in category]? What was that about?"
- "When you look at how your peers in [community/sport/domain] approach [X], do you tend to be ahead of them, behind them, or roughly in sync?"
- "Is there something in [category] you were skeptical of when it first appeared but eventually came around to? What changed your mind?"

**Adoption-curve note:** Chipchase's Range of Distribution is the synthesis application — the participant who tried [X] before anyone else in their network is the edge-of-distribution signal. If you identify such a participant, flag them explicitly in the field notes as a leading-indicator candidate, regardless of which archetype cluster they ultimately join.

## Chipchase Behavioral Probe Principles (Apply Throughout)

Layer these onto questions from R1–R12 wherever they fit:

- **Range of distribution:** Ask for the extreme, not the typical. "Tell me about the time you were most [X] about [category]." Edge behaviors are leading indicators; don't let the "usual" answer hide them.
- **Threshold mapping:** "What would have to change for you to [stop / switch / not buy / behave differently]?" Thresholds reveal where an archetype's behavioral mode breaks and another begins.
- **Workarounds as signals:** "Is there anything you do to work around a limitation with [X]?" Workarounds are behaviors that reveal unmet needs that participants have normalized.
- **Sniff test:** Don't accept the first answer. Follow up: "Can you give me a specific example of that?" The first answer is often the stated preference; the example is often the behavioral truth.

## Step 4A (Scenario A): Map Requirements to Guide Architecture

Design the discussion guide structure by mapping the 12 requirements to a session arc. Allocate time based on project priorities — not all 12 require equal depth.

**Standard arc for a 60–90 minute archetype-focused interview:**

| Section | Duration | Requirements targeted |
|---|---|---|
| Opening and warm-up | 5 min | Rapport; framing |
| Category relationship and history | 10–15 min | R7 (developmental), R9 (first-contact), R5 (motivation pattern) |
| Decision deep dive (most recent significant decision) | 20–25 min | R1 (behavioral accounts), R2 (utility), R3 (swagger), R4 (spontaneity), R9 (conversion), R10 (trust) |
| Context variation and fluidity | 10–15 min | R6 (situational), R8 (fluidity) |
| Social proof and influence | 5–10 min | R5 (motivation), R10 (trust architecture) |
| Exceptions and negative cases | 10 min | R11 (negative cases) |
| KI-generating specifics and close | 5–10 min | R12 (observable behaviors), closing |

Draft the full discussion guide in this structure. For each section:
- Write 2–3 primary questions
- Write 2–3 follow-up probes (behavioral specifics, sniff test, threshold)
- Add an observation note (what to watch for beyond the words)

Include a **Coverage Map** at the end of the guide showing which questions address which R-requirements.

**Priority classification:**
- **High priority (✗ Missing):** Not addressed anywhere in the guide; synthesis will have a structural gap
- **Medium priority (△ Partial):** Present but question design will produce thin or attitudinal data; rewrites or additions recommended
- **Already present (✓ Covered):** Requirement is met; note which question(s) cover it

**For each High and Medium priority gap:**
- Write 1–3 specific question options targeting that requirement
- Name the placement in the existing guide (after which question, within which section)
- Note if any existing question can be rephrased rather than replaced

**Produce the annotated guide:** the existing guide with gap annotations inline and insertions clearly marked (`[ADDITION — R[N]: ...]`).

## Quality Checks Before Writing Files

**For the guide (Scenario A or annotated guide in B):**
- [ ] Every section has at least one question targeting R1 (behavioral accounts) — behavioral specificity is the foundation
- [ ] At least one question targets each spectrum (R2, R3, R4, R5)
- [ ] Developmental context (R7) and situational context (R6) are explicitly addressed — not bundled as "anything else?"
- [ ] First-contact/conversion question (R9) is present and asks for experience, not reasoning
- [ ] Trust architecture (R10) includes a specific AI tools probe
- [ ] Negative cases (R11) are present and framed as learning, not challenge
- [ ] KI-generating questions (R12) ask for physical behaviors — verbs, not adjectives
- [ ] Adoption-curve position (R13) addressed — at least one question surfaces early vs. late adoption stance relative to peers
- [ ] Chipchase threshold probe appears at least once
- [ ] Chipchase range-of-distribution probe appears at least once
- [ ] No rating scales or importance-ranking questions without behavioral follow-up

**For the gap report (Scenario B):**
- [ ] All 12 requirements assessed — no silent omissions
- [ ] Every ✗ Missing requirement has at least one specific question option with placement guidance
- [ ] Priority classification is justified (why is something High vs. Medium)
