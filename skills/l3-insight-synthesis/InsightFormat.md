The exact output format for L3 Insight files and the master insights document, including the insight quality check, the knowledge layer test, and volume calibration.

## Individual Insight File

Each insight gets its own file. YAML frontmatter + five structured sections.

```
---
project: [Project Name]
insight_id: L3-[NN]
synthesis_level: L3
source_themes: [L2-NN, L2-NN, ...]
synthesis_date: [YYYY-MM-DD]
domain: <brand/design | product/UX | organizational | policy/advocacy | other — inherited from the L2 Themes>   # domain registry: DomainScope.md | gate: ../ladder-core/config/synthesis-gates.yaml -> gates.domain_scope_confirmation
---

# L3-[NN]: [Declarative insight title]

## Observation

<What the source L2 themes show — the research basis. 2-4 sentences. Cite source themes by ID. Descriptive: what the behavioral patterns reveal. This is the yellow card — problem-specific observation from the research. Note on the word: "Observation" here is Kolko's — the card naming what the research shows. It is distinct from L1's observation *evidence class* (`l1-findings-extraction/FindingsFormat.md` → Evidence Classes), which names how a single finding is grounded. If a cited theme carries `consent_tiers:` (photo-class evidence), that tier propagates into this insight's frontmatter unchanged.>

## Knowledge

<What the synthesist knows about how these systems work — the cross-domain knowledge applied to interpret the observation. 2-4 sentences. This is the blue card — established knowledge about how analogous systems behave, drawn from design theory, brand strategy, service design, organizational behavior, category dynamics, or relevant cultural knowledge. This is what makes L3 different from L2: external knowledge is introduced here.>

## Insight

<The declarative claim: Observation + Knowledge = new truth about the world. 2-4 sentences. Stated as truth, not as a question. More generalized than the L2 themes it draws from. Less specific than an L4 opportunity area. This is the green card — the new idea generated by pairing observation and knowledge. Written in plain words: see Plain Writing in the Body.>

## What this opens

<The strategic territory this insight makes available. 2-3 sentences. Not a recommendation — a direction. Points toward L4 opportunity areas without prescribing them. Uses language like "the design task," "the strategic question," or "the investment priority," not "the brand should.">

## What would change this

**Counter-cases in the data.** <Who in the cited themes does not fit, and how the insight accounts for each (a narrower scope, a condition) or that it does not (the claim's edge). If the cited themes record none, say so.>

**Rival reading.** <The strongest other explanation of the same observation, with its evidence; why this reading was preferred; where the rival fits better.>

**What would make us revise it.** <The pattern in later research, in what participants would say or do, that would narrow, re-scope or replace the insight, and the kind of research that would show it. No counts, percentages or thresholds.>

## Trace

<The trace-check record: which load-bearing terms were verified against cited quotes; which were revised at the check; any near-miss claim discarded here. Working field — individual files only; never enters the master document or any client-facing artifact.>

## Source themes

- **[L2-NN]** — [Theme title]
- **[L2-NN]** — [Theme title]
[List all contributing L2 themes]
```

## How to read this document

L3 Insights are the abductive leap from behavioral patterns (L2) to design knowledge. Each insight follows Kolko's insight formula:

**Observation** (what the research shows) + **Knowledge** (what we know about how these systems work) = **Insight** (a new claim about what is true and what it means)

Insights are stated as declarative truths — not recommendations, not questions, but claims about the world that carry design implications. They are more generalized than L2 themes and less specific than L4 opportunity areas. They are the bridge.

## The What Would Change This Field

Every insight carries a **What would change this** section, written when the claim is formed, before the insight's prose is finished. It shows how the insight is exposed to the evidence, doing the work a synthesis team does in the room when someone asks "what would make us wrong?". An agent synthesizing alone has no room, so the section has to be written down.

An L3 insight is the best current explanation of a pattern, not a law. Cases that don't fit are expected. They narrow the claim or name its conditions, and they rarely overturn it. This is negative case analysis, the qualitative tradition's own discipline (Lincoln and Guba; constant comparison in grounded theory), not a hypothesis test. The section records how the insight meets those cases.

Three parts, in prose:

1. **Counter-cases in the data.** Name the participants, or the counter-cases recorded in the cited L2 themes, that do not fit the insight. For each, say how the insight accounts for it (a narrower scope, a condition) or that it does not, which marks the claim's edge. If the cited themes record no counter-cases, say so. Counter-cases found at the Trace check (Step 3c) belong here too: the Trace never reaches the client, and this section does.
2. **Rival reading.** Give the strongest other explanation of the same observation, with the evidence that supports it. Say why this reading was preferred, and where the rival is the better fit. A rival set up to lose is not a rival.
3. **What would make us revise it.** Describe the pattern in later research that would narrow, re-scope or replace the insight, in terms of what participants would say or do, and name the kind of research that would show it (a walk-through of recent purchases, a wear diary, a store visit). **No counts, percentages or thresholds:** setting them from a qualitative sample is false precision. If a later quantitative study tests the claim, the people who design it set its numbers.

Where an `abductive-reasoning` ReasoningChain ran, its rejected candidates supply the rival reading, and its "Positive falsification trigger" is restated as part 3 in pattern form, without its threshold.

**Engagement-neutral example.** Claim: repeat buyers stay with the shop, not the brand. *Counter-cases:* two owners followed a brand across shops; the insight holds for buyers whose first frame was fitted, and those two bought online. *Rival reading:* repeat buyers stay with the brand and the shop is incidental; preferred against because owners name the fitter, not the frame, when explaining a second purchase. *What would make us revise it:* if owners in a later round describe choosing their second frame before choosing where to buy it, the claim narrows to fitted first purchases; a walk-through of each owner's last two purchases would show it.

**The tell of drift (the researcher, 2026-11-03):** a count, a percentage, "at least as often as", or a named margin in part 3 means the section has turned into a quantitative test. Restate it as a pattern. The section is meant to sharpen the method, not make it a pseudo-quantitative one.

Audit: `workflows/AuditInsightSet.md` → Audit 3b (auditor judgment, no score). Removal rule (ledger): at each engagement's retrospective, the researcher judges whether the section **changed any insight**: narrowed it, re-scoped it, or gave way to the rival. If it changed none across two consecutive engagements, the section comes out with its record. The same section, adapted to domains, is used at L5 (`l5-framework-synthesis/FrameworkFormat.md`). ## The Trace Field

Every insight carries a **Trace** — the record of the felt test exercised at the trace: the drafted claim set against the actual participant language in its cited themes, at claim-formation time. The check: every load-bearing term — especially economic, quantified, or evaluative vocabulary — must appear in, or be directly supported by, the cited L2 evidence; a term that is not is rewritten into the evidence's own register or hedged to its source. At each abductive leap the participant's words travel inline; a paraphrase never stands in for them. Near-miss claims discarded at this check are recorded, not silently dropped.

Attribution runs in two parts, separately: **who said it, and whose it is.** A participant reporting a third party's shop, sale, figure, or experience is a witness, not the owner of the fact — reported speech travels in reported form, with both parties named, and is never resolved to the speaker by default. Each verified term is recorded by pasting the cited fragment verbatim with its speaker tag and, where different, its subject; a Trace entry that asserts a conclusion about attribution without the fragment that supports it does not satisfy the check.

**Worked example (attribution):**
- **Source:** "DK (F6): MR's 30% female customer base is attributed entirely to staff disposition."
- **Correct carry:** "a 30% female customer base at another dealer's shop, as DK reports it."
- **Defective carry:** "DK's shop's 30% female customer base." — The figure changed owners in the act of being made vivid. This is the characteristic failure of inline participant language: reported speech collapsing into first-person as the claim is sharpened.

The Trace field is a working record: it appears in individual insight files only and never enters the master document or any client-facing artifact.

**Watch items (ledger, per the ratified regression ruling):** (i) the first live body-carry of a reported-speech figure is the natural exercise of the regression's unexercised branch — if it renders with the wrong subject, the inline-participant-language component de-scopes to reported-form-preserving citation only; (ii) first-party body-figure drift counter across the next three live L3 runs (baseline: un-amended 4 → amended 2 → amended 1) — zero across all three, with third-party migration holding, re-opens the register question on this component. Scope note: this check governs insight bodies and Traces; it does not reach coverage maps, and it separates speaker from subject, not attested from inferred — both named as known-open surfaces at install. Authorizing ruling: clause-5 ADOPT-AMENDED, the researcher 2026-08-22; regression adjudication ratified same day on two unanimous chair rulings (Alexander project, `2026-08-22_replication-chair-reviews/RULING-*-regression.md`). The regression's in-draft interception is attested by the run's own Trace, not witnessed — no draft artifact was retained.

## The Insight Title Test

> **Reserved claim verbs:** *validated / confirmed / passed / from the customer's side* are reserved for externally-tested claims — research-derived claims use *attested / evidenced / saturated / hypothesis*. Canonical: `../ladder-core/ladder-map.md` → Reserved Claim Verbs.

An L3 insight title, also called the headline, is a plain statement of what the insight found. It is not a label, a question or a recommendation.

| Form | Example | Problem |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Label** | "Community and Growth" | A topic area, not a statement |
| **Question** | "Is community the primary growth driver?" | L3 answers questions; it doesn't ask them |
| **Recommendation** | "The brand should invest in community" | L4 territory, too prescriptive for L3 |
| **Plain statement** | "Most new riders hear about the brand from someone they ride with, and the brand has no contact with those conversations." | States what is true; could be contested; needs no decoding |

**Two tests, both required:**
1. Could a reasonable strategist push back on this? If it is inarguable, it is probably a summary.
2. Could a reader with no project context understand it on first read, without stopping to work out what it means? If not, rewrite it, however accurate it is.

**Write the title last.** Write the Observation, Knowledge, Insight and What would change this first, then state in plain words what they reached. Ask what people do or say, and what follows from it. A title written first and defended afterwards tends to carry the whole argument in one sentence.

**Plain-statement rules (the researcher, 2026-11-05 and 2026-11-06):**
- **Literal words.** No figures of speech, no terms from the theory imported in Knowledge, and no working vocabulary from the synthesis. The writing is not about the writer.
- **Say what is true, directly and broadly.** Write "riders", not "these riders". L3 is a second round of affinity mapping and is broader than L1 and L2 by nature. The trace to participants is the check. Who the sample was belongs in the limits and the counts.
- **Counts and qualifiers stay out unless they do real work.** Limits go in the Insight section and What would change this.
- **An accuracy fix is a rewrite.** When a check finds a word too strong, rewrite the sentence from what the insight says. Do not add another clause to it.
- **One idea at a time.** A title takes as many sentences as the understanding needs. There is no length cap. Split a sentence that is carrying the observation, the mechanism and the consequence at once.
- **Say so when the evidence is what people report.** "Riders say frames are built for a taller rider", not "Frames are built for a taller rider".
- **Say what is true, not what a brand could do.** What a brand could do belongs in What this opens.
- **A clause with no participant behind it** stays only if the Insight section labels it as the synthesist's reading.
- **No two-part dash form.** Do not build a title as "[what is true], dash, [what the organization has failed to do]".

| Fault | Before | After |
|-------|--------|-------|
| Figure of speech | "A new brand's first ride is the whole case" | "One bad first ride can end a rider's interest in a new brand." |
| Packed | "With jackets chosen by weather rating, a hood that fails in wind is a rating disproved" | "Walkers often choose a rain jacket by its weather rating. When the hood will not stay up in wind, they stop trusting the rating, and they tell other walkers so." |
| Theory term | "Riders anchor on a reference price set by the category leader" | "Riders expect a new brand to cost about what the brands they already buy cost." |
| Stacked qualifiers | "Mostly, and for some riders only in part, loyalty may follow the shop" | "Riders stay with the shop that fitted them." (limits in the Insight section) |
| "These" | "These riders distrust stated frame weights" | "Riders distrust stated frame weights." |

Audit: `workflows/AuditInsightSet.md` → Audit 3 and Audit 3c.

## Plain Writing in the Body

The Insight Title Test's rules for plain writing also apply to the text under the title: Observation, Insight, What this opens, What would change this, the synthesis statement, and any description written for a reader in place of those sections (the researcher, 2026-11-06). The Knowledge section names its framework and then says in plain words what the framework holds.

The body differs from the title in one way: this is where limits, counter-cases and counts that do real work belong. Give them room:
- **A limit gets its own sentence.** Do not attach it to the sentence it limits.
- **No working shorthand.** Write out what the synthesis team would understand from a short phrase.
- **Say whose reading it is, in a plain sentence.** Keep what participants said separate from what the synthesist infers, and do not repeat the same label as a formula.

| Fault | Before | After |
|-------|--------|-------|
| Limit packed into the sentence | "Many answer with how long a frame lasts, though only a few call that value and comfort can outweigh it" | "Many riders answer with how long a frame lasts. Only a few of them call that value. For some, comfort matters more." |
| Working shorthand | "The evidence is from owners by scope" | "We asked only owners about this, so the evidence comes only from owners." |
| Label as formula | "Our reading is that X. Our reading is also that Y." | "Riders do not say this directly. We infer X from what they describe, and Y from what they leave out." |

## The Knowledge Layer Test

The Knowledge section is the most important and most frequently weakened section. It is what distinguishes synthesis from description.

**A strong Knowledge section:**
- Introduces a principle, framework, or pattern from outside the research data
- Names how analogous systems behave in the same structural situation
- Is specific enough to be contestable ("In premium, identity-expressive categories, peer advocacy consistently outperforms paid media at driving purchase conviction — because conviction in an identity purchase requires social proof from peers already inside the category")
- Makes the Insight feel inevitable once you've read it: "Of course — given what we know about how X works, this is what must be true"

**A weak Knowledge section:**
- Restates the observation in different words
- Names a principle so generic it applies everywhere ("people trust people they know")
- Fails to introduce anything from outside the research
- Reads as commentary on the data rather than interpretation of it

**Knowledge sources to draw from:** The body of knowledge you inject is domain-specific — see `DomainScope.md` for the full menu per project type. Injecting the wrong body of knowledge produces a confident insight aimed at the wrong decision-maker. The brand/design menu below is the default; the equivalent menus for product/UX, organizational, and policy/advocacy live in `DomainScope.md`.

*Brand/design menu (canonical source: `DomainScope.md` — the list below is a convenience reference; update DomainScope.md first):*
- Service design theory (frontline worker as brand; moments of truth)
- Brand strategy (implicit vs. explicit positioning; brand equity models)
- Behavioral economics (identity purchases; switching costs; social proof)
- Community-led growth models (Arc'teryx, Rapha, LEGO Ambassador Network)
- Chipchase's trust ecosystem (which trust dimension is failing?)
- Organizational design (what capabilities the brand has vs. what the strategy requires)
- Category dynamics (how premium categories grow; occasion framing; permission barriers)
- Chipchase's essence thinking (what is the irreducible function; what survives context change?)

*Domain-neutral sources (apply in any domain):* Chipchase's trust ecosystem and essence thinking; systems thinking; the platzgeist check (what the organization can actually act on).

## The "What This Opens" Test

"What this opens" points toward L4 Opportunity Areas without prescribing them.

| Right | Wrong |
|-------|-------|
| "A rider-seeding strategy as the primary brand growth investment" | "Meridian should pay influential cyclists to ride its bikes" |
| "The design of a post-purchase relationship system" | "Create a loyalty program" |
| "A dealer experience design program" | "Train dealers better" |

The right version names a strategic territory and an organizing frame. The wrong version names an action. L4 names the actions.

## Volume Calibration

The L2→L3 compression ratio is approximately 1.5–2 themes per insight.

| L2 themes | Expected L3 insights |
|-----------|---------------------|
| 6–8 | 4–6 |
| 10–13 | 6–9 |
| 14–18 | 8–12 |

*These ranges are normative calibration, not derived targets — the audit checks are the principle, not the count. Never split, merge, or invent an item to land inside the range.*


**Under the floor:** You may be writing one insight per theme instead of compressing across themes. Check: are any two insights drawing from the same structural tension? If yes, merge them.

**Over the ceiling:** You may be writing at theme level rather than insight level. Check: does each insight have a genuine knowledge layer — something not in the research data — or is it restating L2 in a new form?

## The Synthesis Statement Test

The synthesis statement groups insights into the underlying tensions they reveal together.

**It is not a summary.** Do not list the insights. Name what they collectively reveal that no single insight names.

Structure:
1. Name 2–4 underlying tensions (pairs or clusters of insights that define the same structural gap)
2. Name any non-tension findings (affirmative positioning recognitions, structural assets, etc.)
3. Write the through-line: what do all tensions together reveal about the central strategic challenge?
4. State each tension's heading as a plain statement, under the same rules as the insight titles: "Brands make claims buyers can't check", not "Claim against check".

**The through-line must be a single claim.** Not a list. Not a paragraph of bullet points. One sentence or two that names the structural condition the insights collectively diagnose.

## Theoretical Basis

**The card structure of L3** (the ladder's adaptation of the vocabulary from Kolko's insight-combination method — in Kolko's original, yellow = the whole insight (observation + knowledge), blue = an established design pattern, green = a new design idea from pairing them):
- *Yellow card (ladder mapping)* = L2 theme (research observation: "I saw this across participants")
- *Blue card (ladder mapping)* = cross-domain knowledge ("I know this about how these systems work")
- *Green card (ladder mapping)* = L3 insight (the new claim: "Therefore, what must be true is...")

The pairing is the abductive act. The insight is not in the yellow or blue card alone — it emerges from their combination. "It is the idea of putting together what we had never before dreamed of putting together which flashes the new suggestion before our contemplation." (Peirce, quoted in Kolko)

**Chipchase's interpretive disciplines** (applied to the knowledge layer):
- *Essence*: What is the irreducible truth underneath the behavioral pattern? The knowledge layer should name the essence, not the surface.
- *Trust ecosystem*: Six dimensions (authenticity, fulfillment, value, reliability, safety, recourse). When a theme cluster is about brand trust, diagnose which dimension is failing — reliability and recourse are chronically underinvested.
- *Platzgeist*: The organizational context governs which insights are actionable. Naming a structural truth that the organization is constitutionally unable to act on is incomplete synthesis. "What this opens" should acknowledge the organizational platzgeist.
- *Research as gift*: Insights that don't change what gets built have broken the research-to-action obligation. The knowledge layer and "what this opens" section together are the mechanism for honoring the gift.
